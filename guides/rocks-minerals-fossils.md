# Rocks, Minerals, Fossils & Gemstones: USA Rockhounding Discovery Guide

Many rockhounding trips begin with a material rather than a state. Someone may want to find agates, geodes, quartz, fossils, petrified wood, gold, jasper, obsidian or gemstones and then decide where to travel.

Use the [Rocks, Minerals & Fossils Explorer](https://usarockhounding.com/rockhounding-map/rocks-minerals-fossils/) to move from a material to the states and locations associated with that search.

## A better material-first workflow

When you search for a particular material, avoid jumping directly from “this mineral occurs in this state” to “I can collect it here.” Geological occurrence and legal collecting access are separate questions.

Use this sequence:

1. Choose the material.
2. Identify states or regions where it is reported.
3. Open the exact state guide.
4. Narrow to specific collecting localities or managed sites.
5. Verify current ownership, access and collecting rules.
6. Check whether the target material has special restrictions.

## Fossils need extra care

Fossil collecting can be regulated differently from ordinary rock or mineral collecting. The type of fossil, the land manager and the specific location can all matter. Do not assume that a rule covering common rocks also covers fossil removal.

## Gold and mining areas

Gold-related searches frequently overlap with mining claims, historic mine districts and active mineral rights. A historic occurrence is not proof that an area is currently open to casual prospecting or collection.

## Gemstones and fee-dig sites

Some gemstone searches are easier for beginners at managed or fee-dig sites because access, allowed activities and target materials are more explicit. See the [Fee-Dig, Gem Mining & Panning Guide](https://usarockhounding.com/rockhounding-map/fee-dig-sites/).

## Identify first, collect second

If you are unsure what you found, document the location, host rock, texture, hardness, luster, cleavage/fracture, streak where appropriate and other observable properties. Avoid destructive testing on a specimen until you know whether it may be significant.
